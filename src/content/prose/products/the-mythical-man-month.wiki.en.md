## The Mythical Man-Month

The Mythical Man-Month is a collection of essays on software engineering by Fred Brooks, first published in 1975 by Addison-Wesley (per [English Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mythical_Man-Month)). Its observations come from Brooks's experience managing the development of IBM's OS/360 operating system. A 20th-anniversary edition appeared in 1995, adding four chapters including "No Silver Bullet"; Pearson publishes the [Anniversary Edition](https://www.pearson.com/en-us/subject-catalog/p/Brooks-Mythical-Man-Month-The-Essays-on-Software-Engineering-Anniversary-Edition-2nd-Edition/P200000000149/9780201835953).

## Core ideas

- The mythical man-month and Brooks's law: treating work as "man-months" — people multiplied by months — is a myth, because adding manpower to a late software project makes it later ("Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later"). The book estimates communication channels among n people as n(n − 1)/2.
- Conceptual integrity: the design of a system should be governed by a chief architect, with organizational forms such as the "surgical team."
- The second-system effect: an architect's second system tends to be the most dangerous, piling on everything left out of the first.
- No Silver Bullet: the anniversary edition's essay argues that no single technology or management technique promises an order-of-magnitude improvement in productivity, reliability or simplicity within a decade.

## Discussion in the show

In episode 010, in the chapter "AI 时代的人月神话：功能越多，产品越好吗" ([The Mythical Man-Month in the AI era: do more features make a better product?](/weekly/010/transcript#chapter-25); Chinese transcript), Orange says he is writing an essay titled "AI 时代人月神话" (the Mythical Man-Month in the AI era). The classic reading, he says, concerned companies with hundreds of programmers; ["today it is not"](/weekly/010/transcript#quote-79e07416165fa20a5f03). If a company's core business is carried by five people and the company wants to hire more people to build other things and add features, ["this is completely a trap"](/weekly/010/transcript#quote-5cbcf812036a18f78089) — adding a feature is now trivial because AI builds it in an instant, "but users cannot accept it; users recognize only one thing." Guizang then summarizes how pre-AI product managers work: "keep piling on buttons." Yang Pan says [Grok Bot](/en/wiki/products/grok-bot) is like that right now; Guizang offers [Instinct](/en/wiki/products/instinct) as the counterexample — "it does not even have an interface," no buttons, always focused on the conversation between you and the agent. Xiangyang Qiaomu sums up: ["adding features is easy, removing features is hard"](/weekly/010/transcript#quote-d3900e5ac5cbc5b28bcd), and Orange adds that when you remove a feature, ["that team's feature is gone"](/weekly/010/transcript#quote-60fdfed35c1d6611d163). These are the participants' personal views and industry observations, not assessments of any specific company.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who wrote The Mythical Man-Month?

Fred Brooks, who managed the development of IBM's OS/360 operating system; the book draws on that experience and was published in 1975 (per English Wikipedia).

### What does "man-month" mean in the book?

A man-month is a unit of effort estimated as people multiplied by months. Brooks argues it is a myth: software tasks cannot be split without communication overhead, so adding people to a late project makes it later — Brooks's law.

### How does "No Silver Bullet" relate to the book?

"No Silver Bullet" is Brooks's 1986 essay, included in the 1995 20th-anniversary edition; it argues that no single technology will raise software productivity by an order of magnitude within a decade.

### Is the book still relevant in the era of AI coding?

Episode 010 offers one perspective: Orange argues that beyond the classic coordination problem, AI makes adding features so easy that expanding a product becomes a new trap. That is a participant's view, and readers can judge it for themselves.
