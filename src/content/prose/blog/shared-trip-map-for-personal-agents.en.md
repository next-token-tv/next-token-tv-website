On the night before a trip, the group chat contains restaurant links, meeting times, and several screenshots of maps. The next morning, one traveler follows an old message to an attraction while another believes the visit has moved to the afternoon. Everyone took part in the discussion, yet they are acting on different versions of the plan.

Personal agents can make that problem more visible. In one trip to Thailand discussed on the show, travelers used their own Instinct agents to help communicate meeting arrangements and preferences, and contributed stops to a shared web map. The accounts of [agent coordination](/weekly/010/transcript#quote-c3f56b43b9e065e260f0) and the [collaborative map](/weekly/010/transcript#quote-b9aed743257e38a71b3e) describe two parts of the arrangement. Each agent can bring suggestions, and the shared page gives the group a place to check the stops.

A map can gather suggestions in one place. It still has to show which suggestions have become decisions.

## Give each stop a status

A useful itinerary entry can include a place, date, local time, and status. **Candidate** means someone has proposed it but the group has not decided. **To verify** means the plan is plausible, while details such as opening hours, transport, or a reservation remain unchecked. **Confirmed** means the designated person has agreed and travelers can act on it.

The presence of a pin should not have to carry all that meaning. A pin might represent a place someone hopes to visit or a meeting point everyone must reach on time. If the two look alike, a richer map can create more confusion. A list can collect items that need verification, while the map shows how the places relate geographically.

## What happens when two times conflict?

Imagine two travelers want to visit the same temple. One suggests 9 a.m. The other suggests 3 p.m. Their agents submit both ideas to the shared map. The system can keep both as candidates under the same place and flag the conflict, rather than quietly letting the last submission overwrite the first.

Once a person chooses 3 p.m., the entry becomes confirmed and records who confirmed it and where the change came from. If someone later discovers the temple closes early, a revised time should return to a candidate or verification state, and affected travelers should be notified. At minimum, the history should show the previous arrangement, who proposed the change, and who confirmed the replacement.

Times need a zone as well. If the group agrees to meet at 3 p.m. Bangkok time, make that explicit on the map and in reminders. Keep the time zone in exports as well, so people in different locations can use the itinerary.

## What belongs on the shared map?

Planning may involve health information, budgets, or lodging locations. The group map usually needs only the detail relevant to the decision, such as “step-free entrance needed,” not the private conversation in which someone explained a medical condition to an agent. Letting that person review what will be shared is a useful safeguard. The map should also make clear who can view or edit it and how access can be revoked.

Shared maps already offer an independent precedent. Google My Maps supports private or public sharing, access controls, KML/KMZ export, and CSV export by layer. The group can maintain one shared link and export the confirmed route for use elsewhere.

A group does not have to solve every agent interoperability problem before planning a trip. If each stop has a visible status, time, and confirmation record, travelers can tell which arrangement to follow even when suggestions arrive from different tools.
