An article reads smoothly, a cover looks polished, a video has no obvious errors. In the past, producing these results might have taken considerable time. Now that generative tools make them easier to achieve, completing the production itself is no longer enough to explain why readers should choose this piece.

Episode 006 raised a pointed claim: once AI brings something up to 60 points, those 60 points are worth zero. Clearly, that is not an accurate conclusion about the score itself. A result worth 60 points may still help someone solve a problem. What is worth discussing is how much of a competitive advantage basic production quality can still provide once more work meets the bar.

## Usefulness and a competitive edge are different

A set of organized notes intended only for personal use may have done its job if it is clear and contains enough information. It does not need to be more distinctive than someone else’s notes or make strangers stop and read. If generative tools lower the cost of organizing information, the result does not lose its value just because others can do the same.

Public content faces a different constraint. Readers can skip it, choose another article on a similar topic, or ask a model directly. Once a piece is readable, it still needs to give readers a reason to choose it.

So “60 becomes zero” is better understood as a shift in competitive position, not a loss of all practical value. Confusing the two would undervalue many useful everyday automations and lead public creators to spend their energy on endless embellishment.

## Quality can improve while works become more alike

An online storytelling experiment by Anil R. Doshi and Oliver P. Hauser examined the effects of using generative AI to provide story ideas. The study found that works created with AI ideas received higher ratings for writing and reading experience, while also becoming more similar to one another. The study focused on a specific storytelling task, so its findings cannot be used to conclude directly that all articles, images, or videos will converge.

One takeaway is that the quality of individual pieces and the diversity of a collection can move in different directions at the same time. Giving everyone an easier starting point does not guarantee more variety in the final selection. The [original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00506) supports conclusions within the scope of this experiment; it does not establish a 60-point threshold that applies to all content.

In editorial work, one pattern worth checking is this: several drafts each feel complete on their own, but when placed in the same list, they explain the same broad problem, use similar structures, and reach almost identical recommendations. Polishing each draft may improve the sentences without solving the lack of distinction between the topics.

## Difference can come from more specific material

Suppose two articles both discuss whether Agents can improve efficiency. One lists common benefits; the other documents what a particular editing task actually delivered, what it missed, and how those omissions were discovered. The latter already provides different information without needing a more sensational headline.

Difference comes from the choice of question and material. A clear failure case, a set of verifiable conditions, or an explanation of where something applies can help readers see which judgments are relevant to them. These things cannot be supplied by swapping in different adjectives, and they do not necessarily require a longer article.

People can gather this material, or Agents can help retrieve, organize, and verify it. What matters is whether the material actually exists and whether its sources support the claims being made. Merely rewriting generic content to sound more personal does not automatically add this information.

## Packaging should serve what readers need to do

Improving production quality still matters. Clear subtitles can reduce the effort needed to understand something, charts can show differences that are hard to compare in text, and a sound structure can help readers find the information they need. Their value comes from a specific use, not simply from being more elaborate than the previous version.

Likewise, adding a voice-over, transitions, or a video version should answer what problem it helps someone solve. For people who need to find information quickly, more playback time may add a burden. For those who need to observe a process, video may be more suitable than text.

This leaves creators with a more stable way to judge their work than chasing a fixed score: maintain basic quality, then check whether the work offers sufficiently specific information and helps its intended readers understand or act more easily. The easier tools make it to achieve a competent appearance, the more carefully creators need to choose questions, find material, and judge what it is for.
