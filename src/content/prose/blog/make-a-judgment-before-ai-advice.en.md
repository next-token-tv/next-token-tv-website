Imagine choosing between two document drafts. Version A puts the instructions first. Version B begins with background and design rationale. Both drafts are available, and the audience’s need is clear: first-time users need to complete setup quickly. Asking AI for a recommendation is convenient, but you could first spend a minute writing down your own tentative choice.

Write down your first choice and the reason. You might pick A because readers need to finish setup quickly, then reconsider if B has more complete instructions. That second sentence gives you something concrete to check.

[Episode 008 raises the question of how people exercise judgment when using AI](/weekly/008/transcript#quote-637b4821fbf12a17f5b9) and [mentions the importance of explainability](/weekly/008/transcript#quote-654e2eb3ccc97e508a15). In everyday work, being able to explain why you accepted or rejected a recommendation matters too.

## What does the study actually show?

In a 2021 study, Buçinca and colleagues asked 199 participants to identify food ingredients and choose substitutions, with advice provided by simulated AI. The researchers tested three interventions intended to encourage more active thinking. One required an answer before participants could see the recommendation. The paper reports that, as a group, the interventions reduced overreliance compared with a simple explainable baseline, particularly when the simulated advice was wrong.

The reported effect comes from comparing the three interventions as a group. The designs that cut overreliance the most also received lower subjective ratings. For a document review, that trade-off suggests writing down enough to check your first judgment while keeping the extra step brief.

## When the recommendation differs, locate the disagreement

Continue the hypothetical example. The AI recommends B because starting with background may help readers build understanding. There is no need to defend A immediately or switch to B on the spot. First ask two checkable questions: do these readers need the background before they start, and are B’s setup instructions more complete?

Compare the drafts. If B includes a necessary configuration step missing from A, and the instructions are easy enough to find, you might pick B and record why. It covers the required configuration, and the link to the instructions can move earlier. If B has thorough background but does not supply the missing step, A may still better serve the goal, though it needs revision. Keep the reason for the change so someone else can check the same configuration step.

## When is the extra step worthwhile?

An initial judgment is worth recording if both drafts are available and you’ll need to explain the decision. If you’re still looking for options, ask AI to help find them first and make a judgment once you have material to compare.

Keep the practice light: one line for the initial choice, one piece of evidence, and one condition for changing it. After reviewing the advice, add the final choice and the reason for any change. That leaves room for independent thought while allowing better evidence to change the decision.
