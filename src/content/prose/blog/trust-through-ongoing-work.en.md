A widely shared joke captures the current moment: with AI, the clerical work of producing documents has gotten easier, while the programmers who write code have gotten more exhausted. Behind the joke sits a more serious question — when execution-level work is heavily automated, what can people still provide?

The answer will not be found in "output." Generating an article, an interface, a video now costs close to nothing, and the supply of homogeneous content will only keep growing. In an environment of supply overload, what is truly scarce is something else: someone who will keep finding you, recognizing you, and trusting that what you hand over next time is still worth their attention. That is trust — and it is not earned through a single viral hit.

## A personality has to be recognizable

In an era when everyone can produce, distinctiveness comes from preferences. Whether one's taste is good or bad is debatable, but the preference itself is the difference: given the same topic, different people will make entirely different things, and some of those preferences happen to match what a portion of the audience needs.

The key is that these preferences must be perceptible from the outside. Taste and personality — without judging either for better or worse — need to come through in what you say, what you publish, and what you build, changing over time while staying consistent. Consistent does not mean frozen: opinions can evolve and style can mature, but viewed over the long run it should still be the same person, not a voice drifting with whatever is trending. This is why "a human touch" keeps getting stressed: content without a human touch is not in short supply; what is missing is a person who can be recognized.

Consistency is valuable because it lowers other people's cost of judgment. A reader does not need to re-evaluate an unfamiliar piece of output every time — they only need to confirm "still the same flavor." Common wisdom from outside about the creator economy supports this too: you do not need to please everyone; if you can sustain a small audience that deeply endorses you, your creative work can keep going. This is exactly the dividing line between ongoing works and short-term traffic-driving — the latter maximizes a single touch, while works accumulate trust that can be drawn on again and again.

"Work" here does not have to mean software in the narrow sense. An article, a podcast, an [Obsidian](/en/wiki/products/obsidian) plugin — any of these can play the role. Some works clear a remarkably low bar to get listed, and soon after launch they gather an active user base, with the positive feedback coming from discussions and usage reports between real people. Feedback like that carries weight that a plain like-count cannot match, because it means people are actually fitting the work into their own lives.

## Trust is a bet, not a law

One claim deserves caution: that trust is the thing technological progress will not swallow. It is a bet, not a law.

Technology is changing the cost structure of trust too. Machine-generated content reads more and more like something a human wrote, and comment sections are hardly short of voices engineered to compete for attention. As the bar for faking "being present" drops, merely looking human is no longer proof of trust — and by the same token, a handwritten touch here and there, or a typo, is not evidence of authenticity either. What can carry trust is what is hard to fake in one shot: a long track record of output, products that survive use, sustained interaction between real people.

Put the three together: works provide a record that can be examined, consistent preferences provide a recognizable continuity, and feedback from real users provides external confirmation. Trust comes from the overlap of the three; remove any one layer and the whole thing collapses easily.

## What can be copied, and what cannot be taken away

The games industry offers an extreme test case. When a console game is fully rewritten to run on another platform, and when remakes advance on a timescale of weeks, the pay-once business model takes the most direct hit. By contrast, services built around accounts, characters, and ongoing operations are affected in a completely different way: a remake can carry off the program and the data, but not the relationships players have built up with their characters.

This observation comes from the games industry and cannot be extended directly to every field, but the mechanism it reveals is worth remembering: the easier something is to copy, the harder it is for it to form a moat; relationships, shared consensus, and continuous service are far harder to replicate. For individual creators, that mechanism points in the same direction — put the effort into the parts that are hard to copy, namely the ongoing work and its connection with the audience.

There is no shortcut to accumulating trust, and no finish line. It is made up of works, batch after batch; each one leaves a record, and every careless one can leave a stain too. As for how to prove your continued presence to a new audience in an environment where machines are getting ever better at imitating humans — that question has no answer yet, and it may itself be the theme of the next batch of works.
