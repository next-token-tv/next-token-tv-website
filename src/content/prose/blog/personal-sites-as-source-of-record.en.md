Someone’s biography is scattered across several places: a social account still names an old employer, an event poster uses a former title, and search results quote another introduction. Each version appears to have a source, yet together they make it hard to tell which information still applies. A personal website can first solve a small problem: giving people somewhere to go to check.

Episode 008 discussed using a personal domain to define one’s own information, with friend links connecting sites. This is a practical way to organize information. It provides an original account maintained by its author, but the number of links cannot establish accuracy or replace checking individual facts.

## Maintain the information people need to cite over time

A biography, project directory, contact details and important articles can each have a stable address. Update the biography when a job changes, explain discontinued maintenance on the project page, and leave corrections on the original article. External links can keep pointing to the same place, while readers can see what has changed.

The W3C’s guidance on persistent URIs explains the value of keeping addresses stable. A personal site does not need an elaborate taxonomy from the start. Keeping years, temporary organizational structures and file formats out of permanent addresses—and redirecting old addresses after a redesign—is often more helpful than repeatedly rebuilding links.

A stable address does not require an unchanging text. Original publication and substantive update dates can be retained separately; factual corrections can explain what changed. Readers need the current conclusion and enough information to judge whether an earlier citation still holds.

## Friend links express a relationship, not a guarantee

Linking to another creator can mean you want to introduce that person or recommend their work. The relationship is clearer when it has a specific object: an author, a project or an article. Presenting links as community navigation is more accurate than treating every connection as certification of quality.

Identity links introduce another distinction. Anyone can create an ordinary hyperlink unilaterally; that does not establish that two accounts belong to the same person. `rel="me"` is a link convention for expressing identity relationships. Verification mechanisms can additionally check reciprocal links, while each service’s rules determine how it uses them.

Even a confirmed identity relationship establishes only the connection between accounts. It does not prove an article’s numbers, product claims or sources. Those still need specific evidence. A trustworthy person can make a mistake, and an old article can become outdated.

## Leave a path from a summary to the original

Suppose a product review is condensed into an image on a social platform. The image travels easily, while its limitations may be cropped out. If the original has a stable address and makes the version, test conditions and updates easy to find, people sharing it can preserve a route back to the evidence.

This is also where a personal site may help AI retrieval: clear titles, authorship, dates and sources make the page’s scope easier for both people and machines to understand. Having a site, organizing it clearly or receiving links still does not guarantee indexing, citations or traffic.

Start with an accurate introduction, a project directory and a few articles with complete sources. Check that old links remain valid as the site changes. Maintaining the content and its provenance over time does more to make an address dependable than adding many badges at once.
