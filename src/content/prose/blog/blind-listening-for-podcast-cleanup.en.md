A recorded interview has obvious room echo. A cleaned version sounds quieter, yet the beginnings of words seem clipped and the voice feels thinner. The editor has to balance intelligibility, natural vocal tone, and the problems that remain. Minimizing noise alone does not settle the choice.

In episode #009, Yang Pan described needing noise and echo reduction for earlier recordings. He also described listening blind to three processed versions and inviting listeners to choose among them. The passages on [the recording problem](/weekly/009/transcript#quote-e0bf9869553048b0e99e) and [the three-version blind comparison](/weekly/009/transcript#quote-534cfb472b56f13b384d) describe the comparison. The listeners could choose the version that sounded best before finding out how it was made.

## What to hear in the same words

Before comparing versions, an editor could select several excerpts from the same source recording: one with relatively mild problems, another with prominent echo, and one containing pauses, quiet speech, or quick consonants. Process the same excerpts in every version so that differences in content are not mistaken for differences in processing.

Listen for three things. Does the room's reverberant tail still interfere with understanding? Have gating or noise reduction removed quiet sounds or consonants at the start of words? Has the voice become muffled, metallic, or uneven in level? A quieter background has value, but smoothing away the character of a person's voice carries a cost.

When you hear a clipped word or a quiet sound seems to be missing, play the original at the same timecode. A detail that resembles noise may be part of the voice.

## Make the comparison fairer

Bring versions to comparable loudness so a louder one is not favored simply for being louder. Give them neutral labels such as A, B, and C in a mixed order, without revealing the processing beforehand. The order can also change between excerpts. Keep the unprocessed source available for checking, while avoiding claims that a small listening session is a formal experiment.

ITU-R BS.1534-3 describes a formal subjective method for assessing audio systems at an intermediate quality level, using multiple stimuli, a hidden reference, and anchors. Podcast editors can borrow the ideas of comparing versions of the same material and hiding their identities. Listener preferences from a small session do not, however, make it a MUSHRA-compliant test or support a statistical conclusion.

## Turn opinions into usable clues

Rather than asking only for a favorite, invite listeners to identify timestamps: where echo distracts them, where a word becomes hard to hear, or where a voice suddenly sounds unnatural. You can also ask whether they normally listen on headphones or speakers to help interpret differences, without treating uncontrolled devices as directly comparable measurements.

If one version removes the most echo but repeatedly damages word beginnings across excerpts, a gentler setting may be worth trying. If a difference appears only once for one listener, revisit the source before deciding. Choose the version that serves intelligibility and listening comfort across the episode, rather than the one with the most impressive algorithm name or the quietest single excerpt.
