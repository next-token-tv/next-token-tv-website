One educational demo is worth describing from a distance: someone asks an AI voice how humans walked out of Africa and evolved; the voice explains on the right while the visuals generate in real time on the left, changing with the content. Interrupt mid-way and ask a different question, and the visuals immediately switch to the new topic. A year ago, real-time generation of this kind was still dreamlike, subject-less blurry imagery; now it already has the look of a produced show.

But the question this demo answers is "can the visuals keep up with the narration," not "is the content worth watching to the end." Only by separating the two can you see where AI video actually stands today.

## Grabbing attention and finishing a story are two different things

Much of the visual impact of real-time generation comes from the act itself — "the picture changes with the words." You marvel the first time; by the second or third viewing it feels familiar. Novelty is a one-shot resource; it cannot sustain continued watching.

A video worth finishing needs another layer. In a short drama or a feature film, the audio, the video, and every frame's transition form a whole; tearing the audio and video apart, converting each into text annotations, and handing that to a model to understand loses a great deal of information, and the resulting edit can only be mediocre. This is why omni-modal understanding models are seen as infrastructure for short-drama editing and automated advertising — they do not generate video, but they can understand it as a whole.

A parallel shift is happening on the generation side. Video models used to compete shot by shot: physical fidelity, prompt adherence, pixel density — whoever looked more real. The new change is no longer aiming to perfect a single shot, but generating multiple shots from one sentence and assembling them into a complete story. This is the same line observed in coding ability: models moving from executing instructions item by item to understanding intent and arranging globally — the finished film is conceived first and each shot realized afterwards, rather than disconnected clips being produced one after another.

## The top works already have authorship

AI short drama is currently the form closest to "content" rather than "demo." What has been observed is that hit AI short dramas appear on short-video platforms almost every week, of a quality high enough to make you forget they were made by AI. One figure that was cited: top AI short dramas on Bilibili can exceed ten million views in a week — that is an observation about top works and cannot be extrapolated to the whole industry; plenty of low-quality filler content exists too, and the "short-drama factories" that churn out volume and the premium works are two different markets.

The more notable judgment is this: finely made AI short dramas should not be read through the logic of traditional short drama; they are closer to top web novels — with their own authorship and creativity rather than a pile of cheap thrills. Authors who tell good stories and can serialize on a stable cadence (say, one episode every two days) are thought to be able to earn paid viewership similar to what web-novel readers give their authors. The "short-drama platform like Qidian," monthly tickets, and usage-based payment extrapolated from that judgment all remain speculation for now — existing mainstream platforms still lean on an institutionalized, traffic-buying logic, and a reward path for individual authors has not been proven.

On the audience side there is one clear observation: the "AI flavor" of early AI dramas put people off, but when they clicked in and found the plot decent, the AI flavor was ignored. What audiences ultimately consume is the story; the generation technology is only the medium.

## One event, many forms of expression

The spread of expressive capability shows up most vividly in breaking news. During the period when the Pinglu Canal was in the news, the same subject appeared in multiple forms — standard blockbuster-style edits, animation-style AI short dramas — and the finished quality across the board was generally high: both the editing, footage, and narrative craft of traditional teams, and expression done entirely by AI.

Half a year earlier, the right to express an event like this was still concentrated in the hands of a few teams with editing and footage capabilities. This is not the progress of a single team but the overall lowering of the threshold for expression. It carries a new problem with it: audience attention is finite, and once supply explodes, the filtering and recommendation systems come under more pressure than the creators.

## The audience decides how far it goes

Low thresholds answer "can it be made"; the audience answers "is it worth continuing to watch." Sustained viewing data says more than a viral hit: some AI dramas are never phenomenal, but their numbers are stable and people keep watching. Stability means predictable viewers and predictable room to monetize — exactly the property that separates serialized content from one-off virality.

As for the structure of opportunity for creators, two opposing pictures currently exist. One holds that lower thresholds let people who could already tell stories earn unprecedented returns — what they always lacked was not ability but a means of turning stories into moving images. The other speculation holds that once content production is highly automated, opportunity concentrates in a tiny top tier that collaborates well with AI, and ordinary creators become harder to see than ever. Existing observation can neither confirm the former nor refute the latter.

Only two things are certain: a viral hit does not equal an industry-wide opportunity, and treating individual top performers as the general level will misread the market; and story quality together with sustained supply — the two things not yet automated — still decide whether a work gets followed to the end.
